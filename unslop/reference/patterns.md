# Pattern catalog

The full inventory behind the top-ten list in SKILL.md. Judge clusters, not lone
hits: one item below can appear in clean human prose; several in the same passage
are the signal. Rule zero from SKILL.md applies to every fix here: cut or
rephrase, never add a fact the source does not contain. When a fix says "name
X", X must already be in the source; otherwise cut.

Words in section 1 are always candidates, not automatic deletions. A single
tier-1 word in an otherwise clean passage can be voice.

## 1. Banned vocabulary, tiered

Not every flagged word is equally damning. Flag by tier.

### Tier 1: flag every occurrence

Keep a word when it is the actual term of art (financial leverage, engineering
robust, a literal tapestry).

| Word | Plain replacement |
|---|---|
| delve | look at, dig into |
| tapestry (figurative) | say the actual thing |
| testament ("a testament to") | cut; state the fact |
| landscape (figurative) | field, market, or the specific thing |
| leverage (verb, figurative) | use |
| seamless | describe the absent friction, or cut |
| robust (outside engineering) | strong, reliable |
| pivotal, crucial | important, or cut |
| vibrant | describe what makes it lively |
| intricate | complex |
| meticulous | careful |
| foster | build, encourage |
| garner | get |
| showcase | show |
| underscore (verb) | show |
| interplay | relationship |
| multifaceted | varied |
| myriad | many |
| realm | area, field |
| nestled | is in |
| groundbreaking, cutting-edge, state-of-the-art | new, or name what is new |
| transformative, game-changing | describe the change |
| paradigm shift | describe what changed |
| empower | let, help |
| harness (verb, figurative) | use |
| elevate, supercharge, streamline (marketing) | name the concrete effect |
| synergy | cooperation |

### Tier 2: flag when two or more appear near each other

additionally, furthermore, moreover, notably, consequently, subsequently,
comprehensive, holistic, facilitate, nuanced, encompass, bolster, spearhead, poised,
unleash, revolutionize, reimagine, alleviate, quintessential, ubiquitous, proactive,
elucidate, endeavor, cadence (non-musical)

### Tier 3: never flag alone

key, significant, important, effective, valuable, unique, essential, innovative,
notable, powerful. Ordinary words. They count only when clustered with tier 1 or 2
hits, or when one stands in for a specific fact the sentence should have named.

## 2. Banned phrases

### Throat-clearing and announcements

"Here's the thing", "Here's why", "Let me be clear", "The truth is", "It's worth
noting that", "It's important to note that", "Let's dive in", "Let's explore",
"Without further ado", "In this article we will", "Here's what you need to know",
"At the end of the day", "When it comes to", "In today's [adjective] [noun]".
Fix: delete the frame and state the point. Also catch the dramatic product intro:
"Enter [Name]." and "This is where [Name] comes in."

### Emphasis crutches

"Let that sink in", "Full stop", "Period.", "Make no mistake", "This matters",
"Read that again". Fix: delete the crutch. If a consequence is already in the
source, lead with that. Do not invent stakes to make the point feel important.

### Deeper-truth framing

"At its core", "in reality", "the real question is", "what really matters",
"fundamentally", "the heart of the matter". Fix: state the ordinary point without
the ceremony.

### Chatbot artifacts

"I hope this helps", "Of course!", "Certainly!", "Great question!", "You're
absolutely right", "Let me know if", "Would you like me to", "Here is an overview
of", "Happy to help". Fix: delete from articles, docs, and posts. Keep them in
actual correspondence when they fit the register.

### Hedging stacks

"could potentially possibly", "it might perhaps be argued", "in some cases it may".
Fix: one qualifier per claim, and only when the uncertainty is real. Keep
qualifiers that carry scope, risk, causality, or legal meaning.

### Generic conclusions

"The future looks bright", "Exciting times lie ahead", "poised for growth", "a step
in the right direction", "only time will tell", "the possibilities are endless",
"The journey starts here". Fix: cut the paragraph; end on the last concrete fact or
the next action. If the source states real plans, use those.

### Business jargon

| Avoid | Use |
|---|---|
| navigate (challenges) | handle |
| move the needle | name the metric |
| low-hanging fruit | the easy wins |
| pain points | problems |
| circle back | return to |
| double down | commit |
| lean into | accept |
| moving forward | next |
| on the same page | agreed |

### Marketing catchphrases

"It just works", "No configuration required. It just works.", "Whether you're a
[X], a [Y], or a [Z]", "meets you where you are", "Ready to transform how you
think about [X]?". Fix: replace with what the source says the thing does, or cut.
Do not invent a concrete behavior to replace a vague slogan. Scan for "It just
works" and "Enter [Name]" explicitly; lazy passes leave them behind.

## 3. Structural formulas

**Negative parallelism.** "Not X, but Y", "It's not just X, it's Y", "The question
isn't X. It's Y.", and the causal variant "not because X, but because Y". One per
piece can be rhetoric; repeated, it is a template. Fix: state Y directly.

**Negative listing.** "Not a X. Not a Y. A Z." Fix: say Z. The reader does not need
the runway.

**Rule of three.** Triads used for cadence rather than accuracy, and back-to-back
triads. Fix: use the natural number of items; two and four exist.

**Dramatic fragment stacks.** "No aesthetic prior. No nostalgia. The old rules were
gone." One short beat after a long sentence is emphasis; a run of them is engineered
drama. Fix: merge into sentences with real connective tissue.

**Self-posed question and answer.** "The result? Devastating." "The catch? There
isn't one." Fix: fold into a statement.

**False ranges.** "From X to Y" where X and Y sit on no real scale. Fix: list the
actual items.

**Anaphora abuse.** The same opener repeated across consecutive sentences ("They
assume... They assume... They assume..."). Deliberate rhetorical repetition an
author would defend is voice; mechanical repetition is not. Fix: merge or vary.

**Synonym cycling.** The same subject renamed each sentence (protagonist, main
character, central figure). Fix: pick the clearest word and repeat it.

**-ing analysis tails.** ", highlighting its importance", ", reflecting broader
trends", ", ensuring reliability". Fix: cut the tail. Give the tail its own
sentence only when that content is already in the source.

**Significance inflation.** "marks a pivotal moment", "serves as a reminder",
"setting the stage for", "indelible mark", "deeply rooted". Fix: state what
happened and let the reader judge the weight.

**Copula avoidance.** "serves as", "stands as", "boasts", "functions as" where
"is" or "has" is the honest verb. Fix: use is and has. Do not rewrite ordinary
"features" or "offers" in product copy.

**False agency.** "The decision emerges", "the data tells us", "the market
rewards", "the complaint becomes a fix". Inanimate things doing human verbs hides
the actor. Fix: name the actor the source already implies (the product, the
authors, the reader). Do not inject "you" into third-person or reference prose.

**Actorless passive.** "Mistakes were made", "it is recommended that". Fix: active
voice with a named subject, unless the actor is genuinely unknown or irrelevant.
Scientific methods and process docs may keep passive. "No configuration is needed"
can stay if no actor belongs there.

**Vague attribution.** "Experts argue", "studies show", "industry reports suggest",
"widely regarded as". Fix: if the source names who, use the name; otherwise cut
the authority theater. Never supply a source. A direct unsourced claim ("the app
is slow") is not this tell.

**Vague declaratives.** "The implications are significant", "the reasons are
structural", "the stakes are high". Fix: name the implication, reason, or stake
if the source has it; otherwise cut.

**Lazy extremes.** every, always, never, everyone, nobody doing vague work
("everyone loves it"). Fix: state the real scope. Do not flag absolute rules that
are actually absolute ("never invent facts").

**Formulaic challenges and outlook sections.** "Despite these challenges, X
continues to thrive", "Future Outlook", "The road ahead". Fix: state the specific
problems the source names, or cut the section.

**Aphorism formulas and pull-quotes.** "X is the Y of Z", "the currency of", "X is
not a tool but a mirror". Fix: rewrite as the concrete claim it gestures at. Delete
a fake-profound closing line rather than rewriting it into a better metaphor; end
on the clearest concrete sentence already in the draft.

**Fake-candid openers.** "Honestly?", "Look,", "Let's be honest", "Real talk" as a
staged pause before a routine point. Mid-sentence "honestly" in casual prose is
fine; the standalone theatrical opener is the tell.

**Summary-recap endings.** "In conclusion", "Ultimately", "Overall", or a final
paragraph restating the piece. Fix: end on the last concrete point or next action.

**Patronizing analogies.** "Think of it as a Swiss Army knife for...". Fix: cut,
or keep only an analogy that is clearer than the thing it explains. Do not replace
it with a new analogy the source does not use.

**Imagine-a-world openers.** "Imagine a world where..." followed by benefits. Fix:
state what the thing does now, using only details already present.

**Listicle in disguise.** Prose paragraphs opening "The first wall is... The second
wall is...". Fix: either write a real list or write real prose.

**Rejected fake alternatives.** Introducing an option no reader would take, then
dismissing it in a clause. Fix: cut the fake option; keep alternatives a reader
would actually weigh.

**Answering unraised objections.** "I'm not saying X", "To be clear, this isn't
about Y" where X and Y appear nowhere else. Fix: cut the defense; keep replies to
objections the text names or a reader would raise.

**Invented concept labels.** Coining a grand name for an ordinary observation ("the
supervision paradox") to make it feel like a discovery. Fix: state the observation.
Do not coin a replacement label.

**Wh-opener crutch and lecturer voice.** Paragraphs opening "What makes this hard
is...", "This happens because...", "People tend to...". Fix: lead with the subject.
"You" beats "people" only when the text already addresses the reader. Do not add a
scene that was not there.

## 4. Punctuation and formatting

**Em and en dashes.** Clusters of em dashes (U+2014), especially spaced ones, plus
en dashes (U+2013) and ` -- ` used as dashes, are a tell. Replace those with, in
rough preference order: a period, a comma, a colon, parentheses, or a restructure.
A lone dash is how people write; leave it. Do not add dashes. Do not run a
zero-dash purge: absence of dashes is not proof of a human, and over-stripping
flattens voice. Exception: the author's writing sample uses them, in which case
match the sample's rate. Scan for the characters explicitly before returning; do
not trust the eye.

**Exclamation marks.** Do not add them. Do not strip a single one from casual copy.
Stacks of them, or a pile in professional prose, are the tell.

**Ellipses.** Only for genuine trailing off; never as a transition.

**Bold overuse.** Bolding every proper noun, acronym, or "key" phrase. Fix: drop
decorative bold. UI labels and a one-time defined term can stay.

**Inline bold-label lists.** Bullets shaped "**Performance:** performance
improved...", where the label restates the line. Fix: convert to prose or drop the
labels. A bold lead-in followed by genuinely new detail is acceptable.

**Title Case headings.** A tell when a generated-looking run of Title Case headings
lands in a piece that otherwise uses sentence case. Match the document's existing
heading case; do not impose sentence case on a house style that uses title case.

**Emoji decoration.** No emoji in headings or as bullet markers in professional
text. Do not strip emoji the author placed in casual chat.

**Curly quotes.** Weak signal on its own; most editors auto-curl. Normalize to the
target format's convention; count it only alongside other tells.

**Question-format headings.** "What makes X unique?" is a tell in articles and
essays. Keep them in FAQs and help centers. Elsewhere, prefer statement headings.

**Fragmented headers.** A heading followed by one line that restates the heading.
Fix: delete the restating line.

**Markdown bleeding.** `**bold**` and `#` headings in emails, DMs, and other
plain-text contexts. Strip markup where it will not render.

**Tool and pipeline artifacts.** `citeturn0search0`, `contentReference`,
`oaicite`, `utm_source=chatgpt.com` and similar URL parameters, `[Your Name]` and
other unfilled placeholders, zero-width characters. Delete them all.

**Excessive structure.** Headers, tables, and nested bullets for content that fits
in two paragraphs. Structure should aid scanning, not decorate. Collapse
over-sectioned text into prose. Independent reference sections may stay independent.

**Hyphenated marketing compounds.** The tell is a pile of compounds (end-to-end,
data-driven, client-facing, best-in-class) used as decoration. Keep the one that is
a real term of art; cut the rest. Do not "fix" ordinary grammatical hyphenation
("a high-quality report"), and do not split a technical compound ("real-time",
"third-party") into something that no longer means the same thing.

## 5. Rhythm and statistical shape

- **Burstiness.** Human sentence length varies. If three or more consecutive
  sentences are the same length and punch, vary them. Do not count syllables, and
  do not engineer a zigzag.
- **Paragraph uniformity.** Vary paragraph sizes; a page of equal blocks is a tell.
- **Trigram repetition and word cycling.** Reuse of the same 3-word phrases, or
  synonym cycling to dodge repetition. Repeat the clearest word; vary the phrases.
- **Uniform punchy endings.** If every paragraph lands a closer, none of them land.
- **The shuffle test.** In essays and arguments, if paragraphs can be reordered
  without breaking anything, they are parallel filler, not an argument. Make each
  depend on the last. Do not apply this to reference docs, FAQs, feature lists, or
  other catalogs whose sections are independent by design.
- **Predictable word choice.** Prefer the plain word over the ornate one, and the
  specific word over the generic one. Do not chase unusual wording for its own sake.

## 6. Chatbot and pipeline leakage

Correspondence and sycophancy phrases are in section 2. Remaining pipeline tells:

- **Knowledge-cutoff disclaimers.** "As of my last update", "while specific details
  are limited". State what the source shows, or cut.
- **Speculative gap-fill.** A sentence about not finding a source, followed by a
  plausible guess dressed as fact ("she likely grew up..."). Say what is not
  documented, or omit; never present the guess.
- **Reasoning scaffolding.** "Let me think", "Step 1:", "Breaking this down". Keep
  the conclusion, delete the scaffolding.
- **Acknowledgment loops.** Restating the question before answering. Answer.

## 7. What not to flag

Restraint is part of the job. None of these is evidence on its own:

- Perfect grammar and consistent style. Polish is not machine authorship.
- Formal vocabulary generally. Only the specific words in section 1 are flagged;
  do not flatten every brainy word.
- One transition word. A single "however" is normal; a pile of "additionally" is not.
- One em dash, one curly quote, one short emphatic sentence, one triad. Clusters
  are evidence; singletons are how people write.
- Dry or bland prose without specific tells. Dull is not the same as generated.
- Technical repetition and jargon. Exact terms repeated on purpose are correct.
- Unsourced claims. Most human writing is unsourced. Flag fake-attribution phrasing
  ("experts believe"), not the absence of a citation.
- Watched phrases inside quotations, titles, code, proper names, or examples where
  the phrase is discussed rather than used. Never rewrite quoted material.
- Text known to predate late 2022.

## 8. Human signs to preserve

When these appear, lean toward leaving the passage alone:

- Hard-to-fabricate specifics: real dates, amounts, file paths, measured numbers.
- Mixed feelings and unresolved tension ("mostly good, and it still bothers me").
- Era-bound slang, in-jokes, and references tied to a time and community.
- Genuine asides, parentheticals, and self-corrections.
- Deliberate choices the author could defend if asked. Slop cannot be defended;
  it is there because a pattern put it there. That question is the dividing line.

Do not add more of the above to "humanize" a passage. Presence is a reason to
leave text alone, not a recipe for invention.

## 9. Register notes

- **Scientific and technical writing.** Domain terminology is precision, not slop.
  Keep the paper's person and voice (including methods-section passive and "we"
  for the authors' own work). If the source names authors, use the names; do not
  replace "researchers have shown" with invented citations. Keep the register
  formal without the ornate vocabulary of section 1.
- **Casual writing.** Contractions, fragments, and asides belong. Do not clean a
  casual register into formal prose; that is over-correction.
- **UI and product copy.** Plain nouns and verbs, concrete behavior over feelings.
  If a line could appear unchanged in any other product's copy, it says nothing
  about this one; cut it, or make it specific with material from the source.
